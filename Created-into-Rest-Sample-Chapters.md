# Introduction

## The Man Who Woke Up on the Seventh Day

Let me ask you to imagine something with me. Scripture does not describe this scene, so hold it loosely, as an act of reverent imagination. But everything in it is drawn from what Scripture does say.

It is late on the sixth day. The light is beginning to soften over a garden that has never known a weed. The rivers run clean. The animals have been named, every one of them, by a man whose voice is only hours old. And now, for the first time, that man lies down to sleep.

Think about what he has *not* done.

He has not separated light from darkness. He has not gathered the waters or raised the dry land. He did not hang the sun in its place or set the stars in their courses. He did not plant the garden or fill the sea with fish. He did not make the woman who now lies beside him; he was asleep when God formed her from his side. By any measure that we would use, Adam has accomplished almost nothing.

And then the sun rises on his first full day, and it is the seventh day: the day on which God "ended His work which He had done, and He rested on the seventh day from all His work which He had done" (Genesis 2:2).

The first full day of human life was the day of God's rest.

Adam did not work his way *toward* that rest. He woke up *in* it. Before he had tilled a single row of soil, before he had proved anything, earned anything or built anything, he was placed in a finished world, under a blessing, on a day that God had made holy. His life began with God's work completed and God's presence near.

I want you to sit with that for a moment, because I am convinced it is one of the most overlooked facts in the Bible, and one of the most liberating. Everything in this book grows from it.

## A restless people

Now set that picture beside the world most of us live in.

We live surrounded by machines designed to save us labour, and yet we are tired. We carry devices that make us reachable at every hour. We measure our days by output and our worth by achievement. We speak of rest as something we will get to *after*: after the deadline, after the degree, after the children are grown, after the mortgage is paid, after retirement. Rest has become the reward at the end of the work, and for many people that end never comes.

The church has not escaped this. In many places it has quietly baptised it. We have turned the Christian life into a performance: pray more, serve more, give more, attend more, achieve more for God. These things are good, but when they are done to *earn* God's approval, or to quiet the fear that we have not yet done enough, they become another form of the very striving Christ came to end. Some of the most exhausted people I know are sincere believers. Some of the most exhausted people I know are pastors.

[AUTHOR: Insert a short story from your own ministry here: a believer, a leader, or a moment in your own life when striving for God crowded out rest in God. Two or three paragraphs. A story of a faithful person running on empty will connect most readers to the rest of the book.]

Augustine of Hippo, writing more than sixteen centuries ago, put the human condition into a single sentence that has never been bettered: "You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you." [VERIFY] He was right. But I want to press his observation one step further. Our hearts are restless not only because we have wandered away from God, but because we have misunderstood what God's rest is. We think of rest as the *absence* of work. Scripture presents it as something far richer: the life of a person who has stopped working *for* God's approval and started working *from* it, sharing in God's own work, by trusting God's own heart.

## What this book is about

This book is about the rest of God. It is about why it was there at the beginning, why we lost it, how God has been teaching humanity to find it again, how Christ has secured it, how we enter it now, and how it will be completed in the world to come.

Three convictions run through every chapter.

**First: rest is where human life begins, not where it ends.** We were not created to work our way toward God's rest. We were created *into* it. The order of Genesis is the order of grace: God's finished work first, our life and labour second. The gospel restores that order. In Christ, we begin again from a finished work, his work, and live forward from it.

**Second: rest is not the end of all work. It is the end of our own works.** God "rested" on the seventh day, yet Jesus could say, "My Father has been working until now, and I have been working" (John 5:17). Clearly, God's rest is not idleness. When the letter to the Hebrews says that "he who has entered His rest has himself also ceased from his works as God did from His" (Hebrews 4:10), it does not call us to passivity. It calls us to stop living by our own plans, our own agenda and our own strength, and to take our place in God's plan, God's agenda and God's strength. We were not made for our own work. We were made for God's work.

**Third: rest is trust made visible.** At every stage of the biblical story, rest and trust are bound together. The tree in Eden, the manna in the wilderness, the seventh year when Israel's fields lay unplanted, the giants at the border of the promised land, the garden of Gethsemane: each one asks the same question. *Will you trust the love of God when you cannot see the whole of God's plan?* A finite creature can never know the full mind of an infinite God. That is why the bond between God and humanity has always had to be trust. And that is why the people who enter God's rest are, above all, the people who believe.

## Why I have written it

I should tell you plainly what kind of book this is.

I am a pastor. I have spent my life opening the Scriptures for God's people, and the ideas in these pages came to me first in study and prayer and then in the pulpit, in a sermon series on delighting yourself in the Lord and in a long reflection on the plan of God and the Sabbath rest. I did not set out by surveying what others had written. I set out by reading the Bible and asking what it says about rest from its first page to its last.

As the book took shape, I discovered, with gratitude, that I was not alone. Others across the centuries have seen some of the same things: Augustine, who noticed that the seventh day in Genesis has no evening; Watchman Nee, who saw that Adam's first day was God's Sabbath; Jewish writers such as Abraham Joshua Heschel, who called the Sabbath "a palace in time" [VERIFY]; and Old Testament scholars who have shown that, in the world of the Bible, a god's "rest" meant his taking up his throne. Where my thinking meets theirs, I have said so in the notes. I offer this book, then, not to replace what others have written but to add one more voice to a very old conversation. I hope it is a voice that helps the weary believer hear Jesus say again, "Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28).

Where I speculate, I will tell you that I am speculating. Moses wrote, "The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but those things which are revealed belong to us and to our children forever" (Deuteronomy 29:29). We may wonder and imagine, as I asked you to do at the start of this introduction, but our imagination must never contradict what God has revealed. That is the one condition, and I have tried to keep it.

## Letting go of the bar

The Dutch priest and writer Henri Nouwen spent time with a troupe of trapeze artists called the Flying Rodleighs, and he once asked their leader about the secret of their art. [VERIFY] The answer, as Nouwen recalled it, has stayed with me. In every act, the leader explained, there is a *flyer* and a *catcher*. The flyer lets go of the bar and flies through the air. The flyer must never try to grab the catcher. The flyer's task is to let go, stretch out his arms, and wait. It is the catcher's task to catch. If the flyer grabs, he may break the catcher's wrists, or his own, and both will fall.

That is a picture of the rest of God. It is not the absence of motion. The flyer is moving at tremendous speed, in full view of the crowd, doing exactly what he was trained to do. But at the decisive moment, everything depends on someone else, and his only contribution is trust.

The Christian life is a life of real work, real courage, real effort and real creativity. But it is lived with outstretched arms, letting go of the bar of our own strength and our own plans, confident that the hands that made the world are strong enough to catch us.

## How to read this book

The book follows the Bible's own story in five parts.

- **Part One, *Before the Garden*,** asks why God created at all, and finds the answer in the sovereign, holy love of the Father, the Son and the Spirit, and in a plan formed "before the foundation of the world".
- **Part Two, *In the Garden*,** explores the rest that humanity was created into, the freedom and trust that rest required, and the lie that led Adam and Eve to leave it.
- **Part Three, *In the Wilderness*,** follows Israel through God's long school of trust: the manna, the Sabbath, the land that "drinks water from the rain of heaven", and Isaiah's call to "call the Sabbath a delight".
- **Part Four, *In Christ*,** shows how Jesus, the Lord of the Sabbath, embodied the rest of God, secured it on the cross with the words "It is finished", and invites us to enter it today by faith, by delight in God, and by walking in his Spirit.
- **Part Five, *In the Body and in Glory*,** considers the church as the body through which Christ still works, and the rest that remains for the people of God.

Each chapter opens a passage of Scripture and traces the thread of rest through it. Shaded sections headed **Going deeper** are written for students, ministers and anyone who wants to dig further into the language and the history. You may skip them without losing the thread. Boxes headed **Guardrail** name a common misunderstanding and the biblical correction. Each chapter closes with a **Rest practice** for the week ahead, questions **For reflection** that work for individuals or small groups, and a short **For the preacher** section for those who will teach the material to others.

Read slowly. This is not a book to finish quickly. It would be a strange thing to hurry through a book about rest.

## An invitation

Before you turn the page, let me ask you one question. Where are you living from?

Many of us live *toward* something: toward approval, toward security, toward the feeling that at last we have done enough. The gospel invites us to live *from* something: from a love that was settled before we were born, from a work that was finished before we began, from a rest that was prepared for us "from the foundation of the world" (Hebrews 4:3).

Adam woke up on the seventh day. In Christ, so can you.

> **A prayer as you begin**
>
> Father, You finished Your work before I began mine. Forgive me for the striving that has tried to earn what You have already given. Teach me through these pages to stop working for Your love and to start working from it. Show me what it means to cease from my own works and to enter Yours. I let go of the bar. Catch me, as You always have. In the name of Jesus, who gives rest to the weary. Amen.

# Part Two: In the Garden

# Chapter 5

## Born on the Seventh Day

> *Thus the heavens and the earth, and all the host of them, were finished. And on the seventh day God ended His work which He had done, and He rested on the seventh day from all His work which He had done. Then God blessed the seventh day and sanctified it, because in it He rested from all His work which God had created and made.*
>
> Genesis 2:1–3

> *My Father has been working until now, and I have been working.*
>
> John 5:17

### Opening: Two kinds of children

Picture two children growing up in the same town.

The first is taken in as a young teenager by a family that runs a successful business. He is grateful, but he is also afraid. He knows he arrived late and has no claim on any of it. So he works. He is up before everyone else and the last to leave the shop. He watches the father's face for signs of disapproval. He counts his contributions and wonders whether they are enough to justify his place at the table. Years later he is still working that way, because he has never believed that he belongs.

The second child is born into that same family. On the day she comes home from the hospital, the house is already built, the business is already running, the table is already set, and her room is already prepared. She contributes nothing to any of it. For years she simply belongs. She is fed, held, delighted in. Then, little by little, she grows into the work. She learns the trade at her father's side. In time, she works as hard as anyone in the company. But she has never worked *for* her place. She works *from* it.

Both children work. The difference is not in how hard they work but in where their work comes from. One works toward belonging; the other works from belonging. One labours to earn a rest he never feels he has reached; the other began in rest and has never left it.

The Bible tells us that humanity was created to be the second child.

### The Word opened

#### Day six, then day seven

The first chapter of Genesis moves with a rhythm like a drumbeat. God speaks; it is so; God sees that it is good; "so the evening and the morning were" the first day, the second, the third. On the first three days God forms realms: light and darkness, sky and sea, land. On the next three days he fills them: sun, moon and stars, birds and fish, animals, and finally humanity, male and female in his image.

And then, at the end of the sixth day, "God saw everything that He had made, and indeed it was very good" (Genesis 1:31).

Notice what comes next. Not another act of creating. Not a task list for the newly made man and woman. A day of rest:

> *And on the seventh day God ended His work which He had done, and He rested on the seventh day from all His work which He had done.* (Genesis 2:2)

Humanity was made on the sixth day, near the very end of God's creative work. That means the first full day of human existence was the seventh: the day God blessed, the day God made holy, the day of God's rest. Before Adam had done any work at all, he was brought into a finished world and a blessed day.

This is not an accident of the calendar. It is a revelation of God's order. God did not create humanity in order to have someone finish his work for him. He created humanity and *then* rested, and placed humanity within that rest. The finished work of God came first. The life of humanity came second.

This order is the order of grace. And as we shall see throughout this book, it is the order the gospel restores. "It is finished," Jesus cried from the cross (John 19:30), and on the far side of that finished work the people of God begin their new life.

#### The day without an evening

There is a small detail in Genesis 2 that many readers pass over. Each of the first six days closes with the same formula: "So the evening and the morning were the … day." The seventh day does not. There is no evening and morning on the seventh day. The text simply stops.

Ancient readers noticed this. Augustine, at the close of his *Confessions*, reflected that the seventh day "has no evening and has no setting," because God's rest is not a day that ends but a rest that remains. [VERIFY] We should be careful not to build too much on silence. But the shape of the text is suggestive, and the rest of Scripture confirms where it points. Hundreds of years later, the writer to the Hebrews would say: "There remains therefore a rest for the people of God" (Hebrews 4:9). God's seventh day was never closed. It has been standing open since the foundation of the world, waiting for humanity to enter it.

That is why Hebrews can say, startlingly, that God's "works were finished from the foundation of the world" (Hebrews 4:3), and in the same breath invite believers *today* to enter his rest. The rest is old; the invitation is new every day.

#### God "rested" the man

Now we come to a verse that I believe holds the key to the whole subject:

> *Then the LORD God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to tend and keep it.* (Genesis 2:15)

On the surface this is a simple sentence about where Adam lived and what he did. But the Hebrew carries a detail that most translations cannot show.

A few verses earlier, Genesis 2:8 also tells us that God "put" the man in the garden. There the Hebrew uses an ordinary verb for placing something (*sim*). In verse 15 the writer chooses a different word. The verb here is *nuach*, the common Hebrew word for *rest*. It is the root behind *menuchah*, "rest" or "resting place", and it is the verb used when the ark "rested" on the mountains of Ararat (Genesis 8:4). In this form it can mean "to set down" or "to settle", but its basic sense is to cause something to rest. Read with that in mind, verse 15 says something remarkable. **The LORD God took the man and *rested* him in the garden, to tend and keep it.**

Do you see it? In one sentence, before sin had entered the world, rest and work are joined together. God settled the man in a place of rest, and in that place of rest gave him work to do. Adam's labour in the garden was not his attempt to reach God's rest. It was what he did *inside* it.

This changes the way we think about both rest and work. Work is not a punishment that came with the fall; Adam had work before he ever sinned. And rest is not the absence of work; Adam was "rested" in the garden *in order to* tend it. What the fall would later change was not the existence of work but its character: work would become *toil*, painful and frustrating, carried on under a cursed ground and by the sweat of the brow (Genesis 3:17–19). We will consider that loss in a later chapter. For now, notice what was there at the beginning: a man at rest, at work, in the presence of God.

#### What kind of rest is God's rest?

But we must go deeper still. What does it mean that *God* rested?

It cannot mean that God was tired. Isaiah says of the Creator of the ends of the earth that he "neither faints nor is weary" (Isaiah 40:28). God does not expend energy and need to recover it. As I have often said, God speaks, and things come to be. When Exodus 31:17 says that on the seventh day God "rested and was refreshed", it is using human language to help human beings understand something about God, the way a father kneels down to speak at his child's height.

Nor can it mean that God stopped all activity. Jesus settled that question once and for all. When he was criticised for healing a man on the Sabbath, he answered, "My Father has been working until now, and I have been working" (John 5:17). If God had stopped all work on the seventh day, then the universe he sustains would have ceased to exist. The Son "upholds all things by the word of His power" (Hebrews 1:3), and he has never put that burden down for a single moment.

So what was God's rest? Scripture gives us an answer, and it comes from the language of the temple. Listen to Psalm 132:

> *Arise, O LORD, to Your resting place, You and the ark of Your strength… For the LORD has chosen Zion; He has desired it for His dwelling place: "This is My resting place forever; Here I will dwell, for I have desired it."* (Psalm 132:8, 13–14)

And listen to Isaiah:

> *Heaven is My throne, And earth is My footstool. Where is the house that you will build Me? And where is the place of My rest?* (Isaiah 66:1)

In these passages, God's "resting place" is not a couch. It is a throne. It is the place where God dwells and from which he reigns. When the ark of the covenant, the symbol of God's throne on earth, came to its "resting place" in the temple, it did not mean God had gone to sleep. It meant God had taken up residence among his people and was ruling from his house.

Many Old Testament scholars have noticed that Genesis 1 reads very much like the building of a temple, with the whole cosmos as God's house. [VERIFY] On the seventh day, the house is complete, and the King takes his seat. God's rest is the rest of a sovereign who has finished building his palace and now enters it to dwell and to reign. It is not inactivity. It is enthronement.

And here is the wonder of it. God placed the man and the woman *in that rest*. He made them in his image and told them to "have dominion" (Genesis 1:28). He gave them a share in his reign. They were not servants waiting outside the throne room, hoping to earn admittance. They were children brought into the palace on the day the King took his throne, and given a part in the royal work.

#### "The Sabbath was made for man"

This is why Jesus could say, "The Sabbath was made for man, and not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27). The Sabbath is God's gift to humanity, a picture and a pledge of the rest into which we were created.

Later, under the Law of Moses, Israel was commanded to stop all work on the seventh day (Exodus 20:8–11). That command was holy and good. But it was, as Paul says, "a shadow of things to come, but the substance is of Christ" (Colossians 2:17). The illustration was the stopping of physical work. The reality it pointed to was greater: the ceasing of our *own* works, so that we might live in God's work.

And so we arrive at the definition that will guide the rest of this book:

> **The rest of God is not the cessation of all work. It is the cessation of self-originated work: ceasing from our own plans, our own agenda and our own strength, in order to live and labour within God's finished work and God's ongoing purpose.**

### The thread of rest

Why does this matter so much? Because what we believe about the beginning shapes how we live everything in between.

If you believe that human beings were made to *earn* rest, then the whole of life becomes a ladder. Every task is a rung. Every achievement is a step closer to the place where, at last, you can stop and feel that you belong. And because the top of the ladder never quite comes into view, you can never rest. Your work, even your work for God, is shadowed by anxiety. Have I done enough? Have I proved myself? Will I be accepted?

But if you believe that human beings were made to *begin* in rest, then everything changes. You are not on a ladder. You are in a house. The question is not "How do I get in?" but "What does my Father want me to do today, in the house he has already brought me into?" Your work is no longer an attempt to justify your existence. It is the overflow of a life that is already secure.

This is exactly how Jesus lived. "Most assuredly, I say to you," he said, "the Son can do nothing of Himself, but what He sees the Father do; for whatever He does, the Son also does in like manner" (John 5:19). No one in history has worked as Jesus worked. He taught, healed, travelled, prayed through the night and finally laid down his life. Yet nothing he did was self-originated. He worked from the Father's will, by the Father's power, for the Father's glory. His life was the perfect seventh-day life: full of work, and entirely at rest.

And it is how Paul described the Christian life: "It is God who works in you both to will and to do for His good pleasure" (Philippians 2:13). The desires that rise in us to do God's work are from God. The strength to carry them out is from God. Even the good works themselves were "prepared beforehand" by God, so that we might "walk in them" (Ephesians 2:10). We walk; we do not merely sit. But we walk on a road that God laid down before we took the first step.

This is what Hebrews means when it says that "he who has entered His rest has himself also ceased from his works as God did from His" (Hebrews 4:10). God did not cease from all activity, and neither do we. God ceased from the work of creating because that work was complete. We cease from the work of saving ourselves, justifying ourselves, and building our own kingdom, because Christ's work is complete. And from that place of rest, we join in God's continuing work in the world.

So the seventh day was not an afterthought at the end of creation. It was the destination. Everything God made on the first six days was preparation for the day on which he would dwell with the creatures made in his image, and share his rest and his reign with them. As we will see in the chapters ahead, that purpose was not cancelled by the fall. It was postponed, rehearsed, embodied in Christ, and will one day be fulfilled.

> **Going deeper: The language of rest in Genesis 1–2**
>
> *Shabat* (Genesis 2:2–3). The verb usually translated "rested" in Genesis 2:2–3 is *shabat*, which means "to cease" or "to stop". It is the root of the noun *shabbat*, "Sabbath". It describes completion more than relaxation: God ceased creating because creation was finished. Note that Genesis 2 does not actually use the noun "Sabbath"; it uses the verb and speaks of "the seventh day". The word *Sabbath* first appears as a noun in Exodus 16, in the story of the manna, which is the subject of Chapter 10.
>
> *Nuach* (Genesis 2:15). The verb behind "put" in Genesis 2:15 is *nuach*, "to rest, settle down". In its causative form (*hiphil*), as here, it can mean either "to set down, leave" or "to give rest to". Translators usually and reasonably choose "put" or "placed". But the choice of this verb, when the writer used *sim* ("put, place") for the same action in 2:8, has led many interpreters to see a deliberate echo of God's rest. The same root gives us *menuchah* ("rest, resting place"), used of the promised land (Deuteronomy 12:9), of the temple (1 Chronicles 28:2; Psalm 132:8, 14), and of the rest God offers his people (Isaiah 28:12). It also stands behind the play on Noah's name in Genesis 5:29. [VERIFY with a Hebrew lexicon such as HALOT or BDB.]
>
> *"Tend and keep"* (Genesis 2:15). The pair of verbs *'abad* ("serve, work") and *shamar* ("keep, guard") appears together elsewhere in the Pentateuch to describe the priests and Levites caring for the tabernacle (Numbers 3:7–8; 8:26). Many scholars therefore see Eden as the first sanctuary and Adam as its first priest-king. On this reading, humanity's work in the garden was not only farming but worship: tending God's dwelling place.
>
> *Rest as enthronement.* In the literature of the ancient Near East, a god typically "rests" in his temple after a work of creation or victory, meaning that he takes up his rule from it. John H. Walton (*The Lost World of Genesis One*, 2009) and G. K. Beale (*The Temple and the Church's Mission*, 2004) develop this reading of Genesis 1. You do not have to accept every detail of their arguments to see that the Bible itself links God's "rest" with his dwelling and his throne (Psalm 132; Isaiah 66:1; 1 Chronicles 28:2).
>
> *Others who have seen Adam's first day.* The observation that humanity's first full day was God's day of rest has been made by several Christian writers. Karl Barth develops it in his *Church Dogmatics* (III/1). Watchman Nee builds his exposition of Ephesians, *Sit, Walk, Stand*, around it, observing that "Christianity begins not with a big DO, but with a big DONE." [VERIFY both.] This book's particular contribution is to tie that observation to the theme of trust that binds perfect freedom to perfect unity, as Chapter 4 argued.

### Living it

What would it look like to live a seventh-day life?

**It begins with receiving before doing.** Adam's first full day was not a day of achievement but of communion. Before he tended the garden, he walked with God in it. Our days are meant to follow the same order. This is why the discipline of beginning the day with Scripture and prayer is not a religious duty to be ticked off a list. It is a daily return to the order of creation. Before we work, we receive. Before we speak to the world, we listen to God. Before we pour out, we are filled.

[AUTHOR: Consider a brief illustration from your own devotional practice: how you begin your day, or a season when beginning the day in rest with God changed how you worked.]

**It changes how we work.** A seventh-day person still works hard, often harder than before, because work done from love has a joy that work done from fear cannot match. But the motive is different. We no longer work to prove that we deserve our place. We work because the King has given us a share in his royal business. "Whatever you do in word or deed, do all in the name of the Lord Jesus" (Colossians 3:17). To do something in someone's name is to do it as their representative, at their charge and for their purposes. The nurse on a night shift, the teacher in a difficult classroom, the accountant at a desk, the mother with a newborn, the pastor preparing a sermon: each can do their work *from* rest, as agents of God's ongoing work in the world.

**It frees us from the tyranny of the outcome.** When the work is ours, the results are ours too, and so is the anxiety. When the work is God's, we can give our best and then leave the results with him. "Unless the LORD builds the house, they labor in vain who build it," says Psalm 127. "It is vain for you to rise up early, to sit up late, to eat the bread of sorrows; for so He gives His beloved sleep" (Psalm 127:1–2). The person who has entered God's rest can lie down at night and sleep, not because everything is finished, but because they trust the One who never sleeps (Psalm 121:4).

**It makes room for genuine rest.** Because God's rest is more than stopping work, some conclude that physical rest no longer matters. That is a serious mistake. Jesus himself said to his weary disciples, "Come aside by yourselves to a deserted place and rest a while" (Mark 6:31). The weekly rhythm of rest that God built into creation remains a gift of wisdom for our bodies, our families and our souls. We will consider how Christians can keep that rhythm freely, without legalism, in Chapter 19. But note this now: the person who never stops working is usually a person who does not believe that God's work is enough.

Think of a swimmer learning to float. Every swimming teacher knows that the hardest thing to teach is not how to kick but how to stop fighting. Beginners thrash and sink, because they believe that if they stop moving they will go under. The breakthrough comes when they finally lie back, spread their arms and let the water hold them. And it does. It always could. The water was never the problem; the struggling was.

The same is true of God's rest. It was always there to hold us. What wears us out is the struggle against it.

> **Guardrail: Rest is not laziness, and work is not a curse**
>
> Two errors stand on either side of this teaching.
>
> The first says: *"If God's rest means ceasing from my works, then I should simply wait and do nothing until God moves."* But Adam was "rested" in the garden *to tend and keep it*. Jesus, who lived in perfect rest, was the hardest-working man who ever lived. Paul, who taught that salvation is by grace, wrote, "I labored more abundantly than they all, yet not I, but the grace of God which was with me" (1 Corinthians 15:10). Rest does not abolish effort. It changes its source.
>
> The second says: *"Work is the curse of the fall, and rest means escaping from it."* But work was given before the fall. What the fall brought was toil, frustration and futility, not work itself. In the new creation, God's servants will still "serve Him" (Revelation 22:3). Rest restores work to what it was meant to be. It does not remove it.

### Rest practice: Begin from rest

For the next seven days, before you look at your phone, open your email or begin any task, give the first ten minutes of your day to *receiving* from God.

1. **Remember.** Read Genesis 2:1–3 aloud. Remind yourself: *God's work came first. I begin today inside his finished work.*
2. **Receive.** Read one short passage of Scripture slowly (try Psalm 23, Psalm 127 or Matthew 11:28–30). Let it speak before you speak.
3. **Release.** Name the tasks ahead of you, and offer each one to God with the words of Psalm 90:17: "Establish the work of our hands for us."
4. **Rise.** Go into your day as a son or daughter at work in the Father's house, not as a servant trying to earn a place at his table.

At the end of the week, write down one way your work felt different.

### For reflection

1. Which of the two children in the opening story do you most resemble: the one who works *toward* belonging, or the one who works *from* it? Where did that pattern come from?
2. Genesis 2:15 says God "rested" the man in the garden *to tend and keep it*. How does joining rest and work together in one sentence challenge the way you usually think about them?
3. Jesus said, "My Father has been working until now, and I have been working" (John 5:17). If God's rest is not inactivity, what is it? Put the idea into your own words.
4. What would change about your work this week if you did it "in the name of the Lord Jesus" (Colossians 3:17), as his representative in his ongoing work?
5. Psalm 127:2 says God "gives His beloved sleep." What keeps you from resting, physically or spiritually? What might that reveal about where you are placing your trust?
6. For groups: share one area of life where you have been "thrashing in the water". Pray for one another to learn to float.

### For the preacher

**Text:** Genesis 2:1–3, 15, with John 5:17.

**Big idea:** We were created to work *from* God's rest, not *toward* it.

**Three moves:**

1. **The order of creation (Genesis 1:31–2:3).** Humanity's first full day was God's day of rest. Grace comes first: the finished work of God precedes the life of man.
2. **The nature of God's rest (Genesis 2:15; Psalm 132:14; John 5:17).** God "rested" the man in the garden to work it. Divine rest is not idleness but enthronement and completion, and humanity is invited to share God's reign and work.
3. **The restoration of the order in Christ (Hebrews 4:10; Philippians 2:13; Ephesians 2:10).** Christ's "It is finished" gives us a new seventh day to begin from. Cease from your own works; walk in the works God prepared.

**Illustration:** The two children in the family business, or the swimmer learning to float.

**Response:** Invite the congregation to commit to the seven-day "Begin from rest" practice.
